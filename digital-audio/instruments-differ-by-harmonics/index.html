Harmonics tell instruments apart

A piano and a guitar playing the same note are the same frequency — what tells them apart is everything else in the sound.

Before you start

Frequency
how fast something wobbles. Your ear hears frequency as pitch — the faster the wobble, the higher the note.
Hz = hertz
cycles per second — how many complete wobbles fit into one second. 440 Hz means 440 every second, which is the A that an orchestra tunes to.
Pitch
how high or low a note sounds. It is what your ear makes of frequency, so a 400 Hz wobble is a lower pitch than an 800 Hz one.
Amplitude
how far each wobble swings from the resting line. Your ear hears amplitude as loudness — bigger swings, louder sound.

Where you meet this every day

Play middle C on a piano, then the same C on a guitar. Nobody mistakes one for the other, even though the frequency is identical — the same wobbles per second, the same pitch. Same trick with people: a friend can say two words down a terrible phone line and you know instantly who it is.

So pitch and loudness can’t be the whole story. Something else carries the identity.

The extra frequencies riding along

A real instrument doesn’t produce one clean frequency. It produces a lowest one — the pitch you name — plus a stack of quieter frequencies above it. Those extras are the harmonics, and they sit at whole-number multiples of the lowest frequency: two times, three times, four times, and on up.

Every instrument has its own recipe for how loud each harmonic is. The recipe is what your ear reads as the character of the sound, and the word for that character is timbre.

This works in reverse too. Any repeating wave you can think of can be built by stacking sine waves — the smoothest possible wobble, one per harmonic — at the right loudnesses. A sine is the only shape with no harmonics at all: one frequency, nothing on top, which is why a lone sine sounds so hollow and unlike any real instrument.

Two stacks come up constantly. A sawtooth climbs steadily then drops straight back down, like the teeth of a saw, and uses every harmonic — 2nd, 3rd, 4th and up — which makes it bright and buzzy. A square jumps between two levels and holds, and uses only the odd ones — 3rd, 5th, 7th — which makes it hollow and woody.

Try it

Open the lab at synth-lab.html#m02, aim at Sawtooth, and drag the harmonics slider up one step at a time from 1, listening after each step. The pure sine hardens into a buzzy sawtooth — one added sine at a time. Then aim at Square and notice step 2 changes nothing — that is the missing even harmonics.
