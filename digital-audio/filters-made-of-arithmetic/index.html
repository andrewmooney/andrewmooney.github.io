Filters are made of arithmetic

Noise-cancelling headphones, tone controls and reverb are all the same thing — adding and averaging numbers in a list, fast enough that you hear the result.

Before you start

Sample rate
how many times a second a recording measures the wave. Streamed music measures 44,100 times a second; a phone call manages 8,000, which is why phone calls sound thin.
Low-pass filter
a filter that lets the low frequencies pass and removes the high ones. It is what makes music through a wall all thump and no singing.
Frequency
how fast something wobbles. Your ear hears frequency as pitch — the faster the wobble, the higher the note.
Amplitude
how far each wobble swings from the resting line. Your ear hears amplitude as loudness — bigger swings, louder sound.
Noise
every frequency at once, at random — what you hear as hiss. Because it contains everything, a filter can carve any shape you like out of it.

Where you meet this every day

Noise-cancelling headphones have a microphone on the outside listening to the plane engine. A chip reads those numbers, flips every one of them to its opposite — plus becomes minus — and plays that back into your ear alongside the music. The rumble and its opposite arrive together and cancel to near silence.

Everything is arithmetic on the list

Once sound is a list of numbers, processing it is just doing sums on that list. That is all DSP — digital signal processing — means.

A low-pass filter — the tone control from the party wall — is averaging. Replace each number with the average of itself and its neighbours and the fast wiggles, which are the high frequencies, flatten out while the slow ones survive untouched. A filter built this way is called an FIR — finite impulse response — because it only ever looks at a fixed handful of numbers before moving on.

An echo is even simpler: take the number from 8,000 places back in the list, make it quieter, add it in. Do that with dozens of delayed copies at different distances and quietnesses and you have a room — that is reverb, short for reverberation. That operation — multiply by a set of weights, add up, move along — is called convolution, and both the filter and the reverb are the same operation with different weights.

Try it

Run three modules in order. In dsp-lab.html#d01, draw a jagged wave by hand and hear it. In dsp-lab.html#d03, smooth that wave with a moving average and watch the frequency response — the lab’s chart of what your filter does to every frequency, dropping where the sound gets quieter. Then in dsp-lab.html#d04, place impulses — single clicks, as short as a sound can be — on a timeline until a plain unprocessed voice sounds like it was recorded in a bathroom. When you want to write these ideas as running code rather than clicks, the Patch Bay’s Code block takes the same maths directly.
