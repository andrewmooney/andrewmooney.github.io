Every sound has a shape

A piano note played backwards doesn't sound like a piano — because loudness over time is half of how you recognise a sound.

Before you start

Amplitude
how far each wobble swings from the resting line. Your ear hears amplitude as loudness — bigger swings, louder sound.
Harmonics
the quieter extra frequencies riding above the note you actually name. They sit at whole-number multiples of it, so a 100 Hz note has harmonics at 200 Hz, 300 Hz, 400 Hz and on up.
Frequency
how fast something wobbles. Your ear hears frequency as pitch — the faster the wobble, the higher the note.

Where you meet this every day

Record a single piano note and play the recording backwards. It stops sounding like a piano. It sounds like an organ, or a swell out of nowhere. Nothing about the frequencies changed — reversing the file keeps the same harmonics — but the note now starts quiet and grows instead of striking hard and dying away. That one change is enough to break your recognition of the instrument.

Loudness over time

The shape of a sound’s loudness from start to finish is its envelope, and it’s usually described in four stages. Their initials give it the name you will see on every synth: ADSR.

  • Attack — how long it takes to reach full volume after the note starts.
  • Decay — the fall from that peak down to a steady level.
  • Sustain — the level it holds at while you keep the note held.
  • Release — how long it takes to fade once you let go.

A plucked string has a near-instant attack and no real sustain. A bowed string has a slow attack and holds forever. Same instrument family, different envelope, and your ear treats them as different sounds.

Try it

Open synth-lab.html#m04 and trigger the note. Start with a short attack — that’s a pluck. Now lengthen the attack, a little at a time, and keep triggering. Somewhere along the way the pluck stops being a pluck and becomes a bowed swell.
