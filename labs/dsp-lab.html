DSP 01 · Waveforms are just arrays

Draw a wave

A digital waveform is a list of numbers. Here is one cycle of that list — 256 samples. Draw on it with your mouse and press Start: whatever shape you draw is looped 110 times a second and becomes the sound.

Sharp corners you draw become bright, buzzy harmonics; smooth curves stay mellow. You are hearing the connection between a shape in time and a recipe in frequency — the spectrum below updates live.

The maths

The playback replays your 256-sample cycle at f = 110 Hz by stepping through it: sample i of the output reads position (i·f·256/sampleRate) mod 256. That naive stepping skips or repeats entries, which slightly aliases sharp shapes — the professional fix is band-limited interpolation, and the roughness you hear in a hand-drawn square is exactly the problem it solves.

Reading it: “mod 256” means wrap around: when the read position walks past the end of your 256-sample cycle it re-enters at the start — that wrap is what makes the shape repeat, and the repetition is what gives it a pitch. The read position moves f·256/sampleRate steps per output sample: faster stepping = higher pitch, same shape.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Your drawing is a list of 256 numbers. Playback keeps a pointer into that list.
  2. Each output sample, the pointer moves forward by f·256/sampleRate steps. At 110 Hz and 48,000 samples/s that is 110×256/48000 ≈ 0.59 positions per sample.
  3. Whatever number the pointer lands on is sent to the speaker.
  4. When the pointer passes 255 it wraps back to 0 — that wrap is one complete wobble of your shape.
  5. Wrapping 110 times a second is what makes it a 110 Hz note. Doubling f doubles the stepping speed: same drawing, one octave higher.

DSP 02 · The sampling theorem, audibly

Aliasing — when sampling lies

Digital audio measures the wave fs times a second. Nyquist's rule: that only captures frequencies below fs/2. Push a tone above that limit and the samples still describe a wave — just the wrong one, folded back down. That impostor frequency is an alias.

Set a tone and a pretend sample rate, and this module tells you what the maths predicts — then plays it so you can check with your ears.

Left: the wheel, really spinning. Right: what the samples saw. Drag the Tone slider (the spin) past half the Sample rate (the camera) and the captured wheel slows, stops, then rolls backwards — the wagon-wheel effect, which is exactly what aliasing does to a tone.

The maths

A tone at f sampled at fs is indistinguishable from tones at |f − k·fs| for every integer k — sampling can't tell them apart. The one that survives reconstruction is the fold-back into 0…fs/2: alias = |f − round(f/fs)·fs|. This is why real converters put an anti-aliasing low-pass before sampling.

The same data, two stories: sparse dots fit the real fast tone and the slow impostor equally well.

Reading it: the vertical bars |…| mean “ignore the sign — distance only”, and round(f/fs) picks whichever multiple of the sample rate is nearest. Worked example: f = 6000, fs = 8000 → nearest multiple is 8000, so the alias is |6000 − 8000| = 2000 Hz. The k in the first sentence counts those multiples: 0, 1, 2, … — every one produces an indistinguishable twin.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Choose the tone: 6000 Hz. Choose the pretend sample rate: 8000 measurements per second.
  2. Rule of thumb first: the highest honest frequency is half the sample rate — 4000. Our 6000 is over the line, so trouble is guaranteed.
  3. Find the nearest multiple of 8000 to our 6000: the candidates are 0 and 8000; 8000 is nearer.
  4. Subtract: 6000 − 8000 = −2000. Drop the minus sign (frequency has no direction): 2000.
  5. Prediction: you will hear 2000 Hz. Press Start and check the spectrum — the peak sits at 2000, not 6000. The samples genuinely cannot tell the two apart.

DSP 03 · Your first digital filter

FIR — the moving average

The simplest digital filter: replace every sample with the average of the last N samples. Averaging smooths fast wiggles and leaves slow ones — that's a low-pass filter, built from nothing but addition and division.

The whole filter, watched at work: the amber window slides along the jagged input, averaging the handful of samples inside it, and the smooth curve underneath is the output it leaves behind. Sliding a window of weights along a signal is convolution — every FIR filter, and every reverb, is this one move.

The orange curve is the filter's exact frequency response, computed from the formula; the spectrum below is the measured result on a live sawtooth. They should agree — including the strange nulls where whole cycles average to zero.

The maths

y[n] = (x[n] + x[n−1] + … + x[n−N+1])/N. Its frequency response is |H(f)| = |sin(πfN/fs) / (N·sin(πf/fs))| — a sinc-like comb. It reaches zero wherever exactly whole cycles fit in the window (f = k·fs/N): a full cycle averages to nothing. FIR = Finite Impulse Response: feed it one click and it rings for exactly N samples, then stops.

Reading it: the response formula looks scary but each piece is plain: f is the frequency being tested, fs the sample rate, N your tap count, and |H(f)| asks “by how much does a sine at f survive the averaging?” — 1 means untouched, 0 means erased. The sin-over-sin shape comes from cycles partially cancelling inside the averaging window; when exactly whole cycles fit (f = k·fs/N), the positive and negative halves cancel perfectly and the output is zero — the nulls.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Take four samples in a row: 0.2, 0.6, 0.4, 0.8. Their average is (0.2+0.6+0.4+0.8)/4 = 0.5. That is one output sample of a 4-tap filter.
  2. Slide the window one sample forward and average again. Repeat forever — that is the whole filter.
  3. Why it dulls the sound: a fast wiggle goes up-down-up-down inside the window, and its ups and downs cancel in the average. A slow drift doesn’t cancel — it passes through.
  4. The extreme case: if exactly one full cycle of some frequency fits in the window, its plus half and minus half cancel perfectly — average zero. That frequency is erased. Those are the nulls in the orange curve.
  5. More taps = a longer window = cancelling starts at lower frequencies = a duller sound. Watch the curve squeeze left as you raise N.

DSP 04 · One operation to rule them all

Convolution — build an echo response

Convolution answers: if I know how a system responds to a single click, what does it do to any sound? Every input sample launches its own scaled copy of that click-response; the output is all of them summed. Echo, reverb, and every FIR filter are this one operation.

The timeline below is an impulse response. Click to place echoes — each spike is "a copy of the sound, this much later, this loud". A short plucked note plays through it on repeat.

The maths

y[n] = Σk x[k]·h[n−k]. One spike in h at delay d makes y a copy of x shifted by d; many spikes make many copies. Pack the spikes densely with decaying heights and the copies blur into reverb — a real room's impulse response is exactly that, thousands of reflections. DSP 03's moving average is the same maths with h = N equal spikes in a row.

Convolution by picture: two input plucks, a three-spike response, and the output — every pluck launches its own copy of the response.

Reading it: read y[n] = Σk x[k]·h[n−k] from the inside: pick an input sample x[k], scale a whole copy of the response h by it, slide that copy to start at time k (that’s the n−k), and Σ adds all the slid copies together. The * often written for convolution (y = x * h) is this whole operation, not multiplication.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Start with an impulse response holding two spikes: one at 0 seconds, height 1.0, and one at 0.3 seconds, height 0.5.
  2. Play a pluck through it. The first spike says: “a full-volume copy of the pluck, right now”.
  3. The second spike says: “another copy, 0.3 seconds later, at half volume”. You hear pluck… pluck-quieter. An echo.
  4. Every extra spike you click adds one more delayed, scaled copy. Ten spikes = ten overlapping copies.
  5. Now imagine thousands of tiny spikes packed together, fading out — the copies blur into a wash. That is reverb, and that is what a real room does to every sound you make in it.
Try thisOne extra spike = slapback echo. A few spaced-out, fading spikes = rhythmic delays. Now click twenty tiny spikes close together, heights falling — congratulations, you've hand-built a reverb, which is all a reverb is.
