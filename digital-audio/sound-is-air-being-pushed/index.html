Sound is air being pushed

Record five seconds of your own voice and look at it — the wobble on screen is the air in the room moving.

Where you meet this every day

Say your own name out loud. Your vocal cords vibrate, and that vibration shoves the air next to your mouth. That squeezed air shoves the air beyond it, and the push travels across the room until it reaches someone’s eardrum and shoves that too. Nothing flies from you to them. Only the push moves.

You can see this. Record five seconds of yourself talking and the recording draws a jagged line that never sits still — the waveform. That line is the air pressure at the microphone, moment by moment: above the resting line means squeezed, below means stretched thin.

Two things you can read off the wobble

How fast the pressure wobbles is the frequency, and your ear hears frequency as pitch. Faster wobble, higher note. One complete wobble — back and forth, once — is what scientists call a cycle, and frequency is counted in cycles per second, called hertz and written Hz. A deep hum might be 100 Hz; a whistle can be over 2,000 Hz. The A an orchestra tunes to is 440 Hz — 440 wobbles every second.

How far each wobble swings from the resting line is the amplitude, and your ear hears amplitude as loudness. Bigger swings, louder sound. Shouting doesn’t change your pitch much — it changes how hard you shove the air. Loudness is measured in decibels, written dB. It is a squashed scale: every 10 dB up sounds roughly twice as loud, so a whisper at 30 dB and a rock concert at 110 dB are much further apart than the numbers look.

That’s the whole vocabulary. Everything else in this subject builds on these two.

Try it

Open the lab at synth-lab.html#m01 and record yourself humming a low note, then sliding up to a high one. Zoom into the waveform until you can see individual wobbles rather than a blur — they get closer together as your pitch rose. Then play the recording back and watch the spectrum view underneath — the same sound sorted by frequency instead of by time, low on the left, high on the right. The peak walks to the right as the pitch rises.
