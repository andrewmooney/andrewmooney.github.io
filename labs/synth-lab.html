Module 01 · Foundations

What is sound?

Sound is air wobbling back and forth. When anything vibrates — a guitar string, a speaker cone, your vocal cords — it shoves the air molecules next to it. Those molecules shove their neighbours, and a ripple of squashed and stretched air (a pressure wave) spreads outward at about 343 metres per second. No air, no sound: in space there is nothing to vibrate, so sound cannot travel at all.

The squeeze travels; the air doesn't. Watch the amber particle — it wobbles around its dotted home mark and never leaves, while the bunched-up region sweeps past it to the right.

Frequency is how fast the air wobbles, measured in hertz (Hz) — wobbles per second. We hear frequency as pitch: 110 Hz is a low bass note, 880 Hz a high one. Double any frequency and you get the same note an octave higher — that's why 220 Hz and 440 Hz both sound like "A".

Amplitude is how big the wobble is — how hard the air gets squashed. We hear amplitude as loudness. A whisper barely disturbs the air; a jet engine slams it. Loudness is usually measured in decibels (dB), and sounds above about 85 dB slowly damage hearing — which is why the volume in this lab is capped.

How your ears hear it

Your ear is a chain of tiny machines that turns air pressure into nerve signals:

Outer ear

  • Pinna: the visible ear funnels the wave inward
  • Eardrum: a skin membrane that vibrates in step with the air — same frequency, same relative amplitude
catches the wobble

Middle ear

  • Ossicles: the three smallest bones in your body (hammer, anvil, stirrup)
  • Job: lever the eardrum's wobble and pass it on, roughly 20× stronger
amplifies it

Inner ear

  • Cochlea: a fluid-filled spiral lined with thousands of hair cells
  • Job: each spot responds to one frequency — high near the entrance, low at the tip — a built-in spectrum analyser
converts it to nerve signals

Healthy young ears hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz; the top end falls with age (and with loud headphones — hair cells don't grow back). The slider below covers 55–880 Hz, the range where most music lives.

A synthesiser is just a machine that creates and shapes these wobbles with electricity instead of a string or a drum skin. The scope below shows the wave your speaker is pushing into the air: frequency on the x-axis timing, amplitude on the y-axis height.

The maths

A pure tone is y(t) = A·sin(2πft): A is the amplitude, f the frequency in hertz, and 2πft the phase — how far around one wobble cycle you are at time t. Sound travels at about 343 m/s, so a wave’s physical length is λ = 343/f metres: a 110 Hz bass wave is over 3 m long; a 10 kHz hiss just 3.4 cm. Loudness in decibels compares two amplitudes: dB = 20·log₁₀(A/A₀) — every 6 dB is a doubling.

One wobble, anatomically: height is amplitude, width is the period — and frequency is how many of these fit into each second.

Reading it: italic letters are quantities that can vary — y(t) means “the air pressure y at time t”, a function: feed in a time, get back a value. sin() repeats every 2π because angles are measured in radians, where 2π is one full circle; multiplying by ft asks “how many circles has this wave completed by time t?”. A just scales the height. log₁₀ is “how many times must I multiply 10?” — it turns huge loudness ratios into small, ear-like numbers.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Pick a moment in time — say t = 0.5 seconds — and a pitch, f = 220 wobbles per second.
  2. Multiply them: 220 × 0.5 = 110. By half a second, the wave has completed 110 full wobbles.
  3. Multiply by 2π (about 6.28) to turn “wobbles completed” into an angle, because sin() thinks in angles: 110 × 6.28 ≈ 691 radians.
  4. sin() of that angle gives one number between −1 and +1 — where in its wobble the wave is right now.
  5. Multiply by A (say 0.4) to set the loudness: the answer, about −0.35 to +0.35, is one air-pressure value.
  6. Your speaker does this whole recipe 48,000 times every second, once per sample. That stream of numbers IS the sound.

Module 02 · Oscillator (VCO)

Waveforms — the raw ingredients

The oscillator is where sound starts. It repeats a shape over and over. The shape decides the timbre (tone colour), because every shape is really a recipe of pure sine waves stacked on top of each other — called harmonics.

Sine = one pure tone, no extras. Triangle = mellow, a few quiet harmonics. Square = hollow, odd harmonics only (think game consoles). Sawtooth = bright and buzzy, every harmonic — the classic synth sound.

The recipe assembling itself: start from one pure sine, stack in harmonic after harmonic, and watch the smooth curve sharpen into a buzzy sawtooth — then it empties out and builds again.

The maths

Fourier’s theorem: any repeating wave is a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Saw: y = Σ sin(2πkft)/k for k = 1,2,3,… Square: the same but odd k only. Triangle: odd k with amplitudes 1/k² and alternating sign — the 1/k² decay is why it sounds so much mellower than the square’s 1/k.

Three sines, each faster and quieter than the last — added together they are already turning into a sawtooth.

Reading it: Σ (capital sigma) means “add up one copy of this expression for each value of k”. Here k counts harmonics: k = 1 is the fundamental, k = 2 twice its frequency, and so on. Each term sin(2πkft)/k is a sine at k-times the pitch, divided by k so higher harmonics get quieter. Writing 1/k² means dividing by k·k — a much faster fade.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Start with one sine at the pitch you want — say 110 Hz, at full volume. That is harmonic k = 1.
  2. Add a second sine at twice the frequency (220 Hz) but at half the volume (1/2). That is k = 2.
  3. Add a third at three times the frequency (330 Hz) at a third of the volume (1/3). Keep going: k = 4, 5, 6…
  4. At every moment, add all their values together into one number. Do that for every sample.
  5. The result stops looking like a smooth sine — with enough harmonics it becomes the jagged sawtooth. Leave out the even-numbered ones and it becomes a square instead.

Build a wave from sines

You can test this idea for yourself. Below, you start with one pure sine and stack its harmonics on top, one by one. Watch the smooth curve grow corners and turn into a square or a saw, and listen to the tone sharpen as each harmonic lands.

The maths

The partial sum SN(t) = Σk=1..N ak sin(2πkft) approaches the target shape as N grows, but near a sharp corner it always overshoots by about 9% no matter how large N gets — the Gibbs phenomenon. A truly sharp edge needs infinite harmonics, which is also why digital systems band-limit their waveforms: harmonics above half the sample rate would alias.

Reading it: the subscript in SN is a label, not a multiplication — it names “the sum using the first N terms”. As you drag the slider you are literally changing N. “Overshoots by 9%” means the wiggle right at the corner pokes about 9% past the target height — watch for it on the screen at high N.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Set the slider to N = 1: you hear and see one pure sine. That is the whole sum so far.
  2. Move to N = 2: the machine adds the second harmonic (if this shape uses it) on top. The shape bends.
  3. Every step up adds exactly one more sine to the pile — nothing else changes.
  4. Watch the corners: they get sharper and sharper but never perfect, and right beside each corner a little overshoot wiggle appears — about 9% too tall, no matter how high you push N.

Dim line: the lone fundamental. Green line: the sum of all the sines so far.

Module 03 · Filter (VCF)

Filters — sculpting the tone

A low-pass filter lets low frequencies through and cuts off the high ones above the cutoff point. It's like putting a blanket over a speaker. Sweeping the cutoff up and down makes the famous "wah" sound of dance music.

Resonance boosts frequencies right at the cutoff, giving a whistly, squelchy edge. This one filter circuit — invented by Bob Moog in 1965 — defined the sound of synthesisers.

The maths

The filter obeys a difference equation: y[n] = b₀x[n] + b₁x[n−1] + b₂x[n−2] − a₁y[n−1] − a₂y[n−2]: each output sample is a recipe of recent inputs and its own recent outputs. That feedback is what makes the response frequency-dependent. This “biquad” rolls off at 12 dB per octave above the cutoff — each octave up loses three-quarters of its amplitude. Q (resonance) is the peak gain at the cutoff; high Q means the feedback almost sings by itself.

What the filter does to each frequency: below the cutoff, through untouched; at the cutoff, boosted by resonance; above it, fading fast.

Reading it: square brackets mean samples, not seconds: y[n] is output sample number n, and y[n−1] is simply “the previous output sample”. The letters b₀, b₁, a₁… are fixed mixing amounts (coefficients) chosen from your cutoff and Q — the whole filter is five multiplications and four additions per sample.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. To make output sample number 100, the filter gathers five numbers: input samples 100, 99 and 98, and its own earlier answers — output samples 99 and 98.
  2. Each is multiplied by its own fixed amount (the b and a coefficients — chosen once from your cutoff and resonance knobs).
  3. Add the input parts, subtract the output parts. That single number is output sample 100.
  4. Slide forward one sample and repeat, forever — 48,000 times a second.
  5. The magic is the “own earlier answers” part: because the filter feeds itself back, some frequencies build up and others cancel. That is all a filter is.

Module 04 · Envelope (EG)

Envelopes — shaping loudness over time

Real instruments don't switch on and off like a light. A piano note attacks instantly then fades; a violin note swells in slowly. Synths copy this with an ADSR envelope:

Attack — time to reach full volume. Decay — time to fall to the sustain level. Sustain — the level held while the key is down. Release — fade-out time after letting go.

One note, in real time: the amber playhead climbs the attack, falls through the decay, rides the sustain while the key (grey bar) is held, then releases. Move the sliders below — the shape and the speed follow them.

The maths

Attack is a straight ramp. Decay and release are exponential: the level approaches its target like e^(−t/τ), never quite arriving — after time τ it has covered 63%, after 3τ about 95%. Exponential curves are used because real vibrating objects lose a fixed fraction of their energy per instant, so a plucked string’s fade is exponential too; a linear fade sounds artificial.

Reading it: e is a constant, about 2.718, that mathematics uses for natural growth and decay. e^(−t/τ) reads “the fraction still remaining after time t”: at t = τ (the Greek letter tau, the time constant) about 37% remains; at 3τ about 5%. Bigger τ = slower fade. The minus sign is what makes it decay instead of grow.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Say the release time constant τ = 0.1 seconds and the note is at full volume, 1.0.
  2. After 0.1 s (one τ), about 37% is left: volume 0.37.
  3. After 0.2 s (two τ), 37% of 37% ≈ 14% is left.
  4. After 0.3 s, about 5%. Each τ keeps the same fraction, not the same amount — that is what “exponential” means.
  5. The attack is different: a plain straight climb from 0 to full over the attack time. Rise straight, fall by fractions.

Module 05 · LFO

Modulation — making sound move

An LFO (low-frequency oscillator) is an oscillator too slow to hear — instead of making sound, it wiggles other knobs for you, automatically, several times a second.

Point it at pitch and you get vibrato (like a singer's wobble). Point it at volume and you get tremolo. Point it at the filter cutoff and you get that "wub wub" of dubstep.

Cause and effect: the amber dot rides the slow LFO on top, and the fast wave underneath squeezes together (pitch up) and stretches apart (pitch down) exactly in step — vibrato, drawn.

The maths

Modulation is a function of a function: the target parameter becomes p(t) = p₀ + d·sin(2πrt) with rate r and depth d. Wobbling pitch this way at audio rates (fast enough to hear) stops sounding like vibrato and grows sidebands — that is exactly Module 08’s FM synthesis; the LFO and the FM modulator are the same machine at different speeds.

Reading it: “function of a function” just means one wave’s output is plugged in where another wave’s knob would be: p(t) is the knob position over time, p₀ the resting position (the subscript zero marks a starting value), d how far it swings, r how fast.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Say the pitch knob rests at p₀ = 330 Hz, depth d = 30 Hz, and the LFO rate is r = 2 wobbles per second.
  2. At time t = 0: sin(0) = 0, so the pitch is 330 + 30×0 = 330 Hz. No change yet.
  3. At t = 0.125 s the LFO is a quarter through its wobble: sin gives 1, so pitch = 330 + 30 = 360 Hz. Highest point.
  4. At t = 0.375 s sin gives −1: pitch = 330 − 30 = 300 Hz. Lowest point.
  5. Round and round, twice per second — you hear the pitch swing 300…360 as vibrato. Point the same maths at volume instead and it is tremolo.

Module 07 · From volts to numbers

How digital audio works

An analog synth's signal is a smooth, continuous voltage. A digital synth (or any computer) stores sound as a list of numbers: it measures the wave thousands of times per second (the sample rate) and rounds each measurement to the nearest step (the bit depth).

Take enough samples — CDs use 44,100 per second — and your ear can't tell the difference. Take too few, and the wave gets blocky and frequencies get misread, a problem called aliasing. This is the maths behind DSP (digital signal processing): everything a digital synth does — oscillators, filters, effects — is arithmetic on that list of numbers.

The same wave, measured: with plenty of measurements per cycle the staircase hugs the smooth curve; as the rate falls the steps grow into chunky blocks — this is what the sliders in the demo below are doing.

The maths

Nyquist’s theorem: to capture frequency f you need a sample rate above 2f — anything higher folds back as an alias at fs − f. CD audio’s 44,100 Hz covers hearing’s ~20 kHz ceiling with margin. Bit depth sets the noise floor: each bit adds ~6.02 dB of signal-to-noise, so 8 bits ≈ 48 dB (audibly gritty) and 16 bits ≈ 96 dB (transparent).

Why aliasing happens: the green measurement dots sit perfectly on BOTH waves. Once sampled, the fast tone and the slow one are the same data.

Reading it: fs is the sample rate (the s subscript = “sampling”). The folding rule in numbers: sample a 6000 Hz tone at 8000 Hz and you get |6000 − 8000| = 2000 Hz — the wave you hear is the mirror image around the halfway point fs/2. The ~6 dB-per-bit rule comes from each extra bit halving the size of the rounding error.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Sample rate first. At fs = 8000 measurements per second, the fastest wobble you can honestly capture is 4000 per second — you need at least two measurements per wobble (one up, one down).
  2. Feed in a 6000 Hz tone anyway. The measurements can’t keep up, and the dots they record trace out a slower wave.
  3. Which one? Distance from the nearest multiple of 8000: the nearest is 8000 itself, and 8000 − 6000 = 2000. You hear 2000 Hz. Wrong note, confidently played.
  4. Bit depth is the other axis. With 8 bits you get 256 volume steps; every measurement gets rounded to the nearest step.
  5. The rounding errors are tiny random mistakes — heard as hiss. Each extra bit doubles the number of steps, halves the mistakes, and buys about 6 dB more silence beneath the music.

Module 08 · FM Synthesis

FM — one oscillator shaking another

Everything so far has been subtractive: start bright, filter down. FM (frequency modulation) builds tone the opposite way — a modulator oscillator wobbles the pitch of a carrier oscillator so fast that the wobble itself becomes new harmonics.

The ratio between the two frequencies decides the character: whole-number ratios sound musical and harmonic; in-between ratios sound metallic and bell-like. The amount decides how bright it gets. This is how the Yamaha DX7 — the best-selling synth of the 1980s — made every electric piano and bell you heard on the radio.

The maths

FM: y(t) = sin(2πfct + I·sin(2πfmt)). The output contains sidebands at fc ± kfm, with amplitudes given by Bessel functions of the index I — more index, more sidebands, brighter sound. When fm/fc is a whole number the sidebands land on harmonics (musical); otherwise they fall between (bells and metal).

What FM puts in the spectrum: the carrier, plus sideband pairs marching outward in steps of the modulator frequency.

Reading it: the unusual part is a sin() inside another sin(): the inner one wiggles the outer one’s angle. fc and fm are the carrier and modulator frequencies (the subscripts are just names). I, the index, has no units — it measures how hard the phase is pushed. The ± in fc ± kfm means sidebands appear in pairs, one above and one below the carrier, at every whole-number step k.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Take a carrier sine at 220 Hz — that is the note you would hear alone.
  2. Now wobble its angle with a second sine, the modulator, at 440 Hz. Not its volume — its timing: pushed slightly ahead, then slightly behind, 440 times a second.
  3. Wobbling the timing that fast doesn’t sound like vibrato — it invents new frequencies: at 220±440, 220±880, 220±1320… — one pair per step, spaced by the modulator’s 440.
  4. Turn the Amount up and the timing gets pushed harder: more pairs appear, the sound gets brighter. That is the index I.
  5. Because 440 is exactly 2 × 220, all those new frequencies land on musical harmonics — it sounds like an instrument. Set the ratio to 3.5 and they land between — it sounds like a bell.

Watch the spectrum sprout new harmonic bars as you raise the FM amount — no filter involved.

Module 09 · Wavetable

Wavetable — morphing between shapes

Module 02 gave you four fixed waveforms. A wavetable synth stores a whole table of single-cycle shapes and lets you morph smoothly between them while the note plays — the harmonic recipe itself becomes a knob you can turn.

Invented on the PPG Wave in the late 1970s and everywhere today: Serum, Vital and most modern EDM basses are wavetables being scanned by envelopes and LFOs. The movement you hear in those sounds is exactly what the Morph slider does here.

The maths

Each table position stores a harmonic recipe ak. Morphing at position m between frames A and B interpolates every harmonic: ak = (1−m)Ak + mBk, and the waveform is rebuilt from the blended recipe. Morphing recipes (spectra) sounds smooth where crossfading raw waveforms can cancel and comb.

Reading it: ak means “the volume of harmonic number k” — one number per harmonic, the whole list being the recipe. The blend (1−m)Ak + mBk is a weighted average: at m = 0 you get pure recipe A, at m = 1 pure B, at m = 0.5 an equal mix — m is exactly the Morph slider, scaled 0 to 1.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Each stored shape is just a list: “harmonic 1 this loud, harmonic 2 this loud…”.
  2. Say shape A has harmonic 3 at volume 0.2 and shape B has it at 0.6.
  3. With the Morph slider halfway (m = 0.5), blend them: half of 0.2 plus half of 0.6 = 0.4.
  4. Do that same blend for every harmonic in the list — one weighted average each.
  5. Rebuild the waveform from the blended list and play it. Slide the knob and the recipe — and the sound — glides between the two shapes.

Watch the spectrum bars slide between recipes as you morph — sine's single bar grows into saw's full staircase.

Module 10 · Granular

Granular — sound as tiny grains

Granular synthesis takes a recording, chops it into grains a few dozen milliseconds long, and replays them in a stream — hundreds per second, overlapping, from wherever in the recording you point. Big grains sound like stuttering echoes; tiny dense grains melt into a continuous texture.

This is how film sound designers build drones from a single word, and how your phone slows a voice memo down without making it deeper — time-stretching is granular playback with the position crawling forward slower than real time.

The maths

Each grain is the source multiplied by a short window w(t) that fades in and out (ours is triangular; Hann, cos², is the classic). Windowing prevents clicks at the grain edges, but shorter grains smear frequency: a grain of length T can’t resolve frequencies closer than about 1/T Hz — the same time-frequency trade-off behind the uncertainty principle.

Grains on a timeline: every snippet fades in and out, and the overlaps hide the joins.

Reading it: w(t) is the window — a volume curve multiplied onto the grain so it fades in and out instead of clicking. The trade-off in numbers: a 20 ms grain (T = 0.02 s) blurs frequencies by about 1/0.02 = 50 Hz, so two tones 30 Hz apart become indistinguishable inside it. Long grains hear pitch precisely but smear time; short grains the reverse.

Step by step — with real numbers:

  1. Cut a 50-millisecond snippet out of the recording — one grain.
  2. Multiply it by a fade-in/fade-out curve (the window) so it starts and ends at zero — no clicks.
  3. Play it. Before it finishes, fire the next grain from (almost) the same spot, overlapping.
  4. Dozens of overlapping windowed snippets per second fuse into one continuous texture.
  5. The catch: a 50 ms snippet can’t tell apart frequencies closer than about 1/0.05 = 20 Hz. Shrink the grains and the pitch gets blurrier — you trade time-detail for pitch-detail.

Module 11 · Families

Analog vs digital vs hybrid

All three families use the same ideas you've just learned — oscillators, filters, envelopes, modulation. They differ in what the signal is made of while it travels through the synth.

Analog

  • Signal: continuous voltage in real circuits
  • Sound: warm, slightly imperfect — components drift
  • Strength: smooth, organic character
  • Weakness: can't save patches easily (originally), goes out of tune
Minimoog · Prophet-5 · Korg MS-20

Digital

  • Signal: numbers processed by chips (DSP)
  • Sound: precise, clean — can be anything
  • Strength: FM, wavetables, samples, total recall of patches
  • Weakness: early ones sounded cold; aliasing if done badly
Yamaha DX7 · Roland D-50 · every softsynth

Hybrid

  • Signal: digital oscillators → real analog filters
  • Sound: digital flexibility with analog warmth
  • Strength: best of both, stable tuning, saved patches
  • Weakness: more complex to design and build
Ensoniq ESQ-1 · Novation Peak · Prophet VS
