When sampling lies

Sample too slowly and you don't just lose a frequency — you record a different one that was never there. The Nyquist limit, and the wheels that spin backwards.

Before you start

Sample rate
how many times a second a recording measures the wave. Streamed music measures 44,100 times a second; a phone call manages 8,000, which is why phone calls sound thin.
Hz = hertz
cycles per second — how many complete wobbles fit into one second. 440 Hz means 440 every second, which is the A that an orchestra tunes to.
Frequency
how fast something wobbles. Your ear hears frequency as pitch — the faster the wobble, the higher the note.
Filter
something that removes part of the frequency range and leaves the rest. A wall is a filter — the bass gets through and the cymbals do not.

Where you meet this every day

Film a car pulling away and watch its wheels on the replay. They speed up, slow down, stop, then start turning backwards while the car keeps moving forwards. Nothing is wrong with the wheel. The camera takes 25 pictures a second, the wheel spins faster than that, and between one frame and the next a spoke has travelled almost a full gap. The footage is a perfectly honest record of a rotation that never happened.

The Nyquist limit

Sampling audio has the same failure. Every recording sets how many measurements it takes per second; the rule that falls out of it is that you can only capture frequencies below half the sample rate. That halfway point is the Nyquist limit, after the engineer who worked the rule out.

Push a tone above it and it does not vanish. It folds back down and reappears as a lower frequency — a 5,000 Hz tone sampled 8,000 times a second comes back as a 3,000 Hz tone that was never played. The fake tone is called an alias, and the whole failure is aliasing. This is why converters — the chips that turn a wave into numbers — put a filter in front of the measuring: cut the too-fast frequencies before they get sampled, because once they are in the list of numbers there is no telling them apart from real ones.

Try it

Open dsp-lab.html#d02 and raise the tone slowly. As you cross half the sample rate, the lab predicts the folded frequency on screen before playing it. Read the number, then listen — the pitch you hear falls while the one you set rises.
